**1/**
I scored my own SaaS idea 13/25 and killed it.

Saved myself two months of building the wrong thing.

Here's the 5-axis scoring system I use before writing any code:

**2/**
First, the mistake this fixes.

You find a gap no competitor fills. You get excited. You build.

Three months later: the gap was empty because it wasn't worth filling.

Finding gaps is easy. Knowing which ones are defensible is the whole game.

**3/**
The five axes. Score each 1-5:

• Data moat — improves with usage?
• Network effects — better with more users?
• Switching cost — painful to leave?
• Distribution — do you already reach this buyer?
• Technical complexity — hard to clone fast?

**4/**
How to read your total:

Under 10 → it's a feature, not a company
10-17 → viable niche, expect a competitor in 6-12 months
18+ → defensibility compounds, worth the build

**5/**
My worked example:

Idea — an AI tool writing LinkedIn build-in-public posts from your actual
GitHub commits and Stripe MRR, instead of from a blank text box.

I checked 12 existing tools. Not one touches real product data. Gap
confirmed.

**6/**
The scores:

Data moat: 4 (compounds with every commit)
Network effects: 1 (single-player, none)
Switching cost: 3
Distribution: 2 (zero reach into that audience)
Technical complexity: 3 (GitHub + Stripe API — weeks, not months)

13/25.

**7/**
Real wedge. But: no distribution, and buildable by anyone in a quarter.

For someone who already has an indie-hacker audience, 13 is a green light.

For me, without one, it wasn't.

That's the decision the score made for me.

**8/**
The uncomfortable part:

Your own ideas will usually score lower than you want them to.

If you're consistently landing 20+, you're grading generously. That's the
main way this fails when you run it on yourself.

**9/**
One research warning from doing this:

A lot of "best tools in [category]" articles are published by the company
that ranks #1 in them.

Always check who owns the domain before trusting a ranking.
