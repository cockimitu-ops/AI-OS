# Example Run-Through — A Complete Video, Start to Finish

All six modules run on one story. The output at the end is a script you could
paste into an editor today.

**The premise chosen:** a historical survival account — the 1972 Andes crash
is public record, widely documented, no private individuals exposed. Working
in the "survival stories" niche.

---

## Module 1 — Source Mining

Ten premises were generated; here are the top three by stop-reading
difficulty, and why the winner won.

| Premise | First reversal | Not seen coming | Difficulty to stop |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rugby team crashes in Andes; survivors hear their own rescue called off on a radio | Rescue abandoned at day 8 | Two of them walked out over a mountain range with no equipment | **Very high** |
| Lighthouse keeper vanishes, logbook continues in his handwriting for 3 days | The handwriting isn't his | Never resolved | High |
| Man survives 76 days adrift after boat sinks | Rescue ship passes without seeing him | He caught rain in a shoe | High |

**Winner:** the Andes crash. It has three reversals rather than two (crash →
rescue called off → the walk out), each escalating, and the outcome isn't
guessable from the setup. The lighthouse story has stronger mystery but no
resolution, which kills the ending.

**Sourcing check:** all public record, extensively documented, decades old,
no living private individual exposed by the retelling.

## Module 2 — Hook Engineering

Eight lines generated. All eight, with tier labels — including the ones cut,
because seeing the rejects is half the lesson:

| # | Line | Tier | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | They heard the radio say the search was over. | 3 — Disturbing mystery | **Kept** |
| 2 | Twenty nine people survived the crash. Sixteen came home. | 4 — High stakes | Kept as backup |
| 3 | The rescue planes stopped looking on day eight. | 1 — Immediate danger | Strong |
| 4 | Imagine being told nobody is coming for you. | — | **Cut — starts with "Imagine"** |
| 5 | This is the story of a plane crash in the Andes. | — | **Cut — "This is the story of"** |
| 6 | What would you do if rescue gave up? | — | **Cut — rhetorical question** |
| 7 | Their plane hit a mountain at twelve thousand feet. | 1 — Immediate danger | Strong |
| 8 | The survivors made a decision nobody talks about. | 3 — Disturbing mystery | Kept as backup |

**Chosen: line 1.** It's a tier-3 disturbing mystery that implies the danger
without stating it, and it front-loads the single most arresting fact — they
*heard* it happen. Line 7 is a tier-1 and technically ranks higher, but it
describes an event the viewer can picture immediately, which closes the loop
rather than opening one.

**Note that three of eight were auto-rejected** by the rule list. That's
typical, and it's why the rules exist — those are the defaults a model
reaches for.

## Module 4 — TTS-Native Script

**268 words. Estimated 112 seconds at standard pace.** Paste directly into
your TTS tool.

```
They heard the radio say the search was over.

October, nineteen seventy two. A plane carrying a rugby team hits a
mountain in the Andes at twelve thousand feet. It breaks apart on
impact. The survivors are trapped in the fuselage, in snow, with no
winter clothing and almost no food.

One thing still works. A transistor radio.

For eight days they listen for news of the search. On the eighth day,
they hear it. The search has been called off. The authorities have
decided nobody could still be alive up there.

They are alive. And now nobody is looking.

The temperature drops to thirty below at night. They melt snow using
sheets of metal from the wreckage. Weeks pass.

Then an avalanche hits the fuselage while they sleep. It buries them
inside their own shelter. Eight more people die that night.

By December, two of them make a decision. If nobody is coming, they
will walk out.

They have no map. No climbing equipment. No idea which direction leads
anywhere. They are wearing clothes taken from the dead.

They climb west, because west looks lower. It is not lower. They are
climbing directly into the highest part of the range.

For ten days they walk. They sleep in the open. They cross peaks that
professional climbers train years to attempt.

On the tenth day, they see something below them. A river. Green
valleys. And on the far bank, a man on horseback.

He throws a note across the water wrapped around a stone.

Sixteen people came home. Seventy two days after everyone stopped
looking for them.
```

**Rule check:** all numbers spelled out. Longest sentence 14 words. No
emojis, parentheses, or em dashes. Commas placed only for audible pauses.

## Module 5 — Visual and Audio Layer

| Time | Overlay text | Visual | SFX |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | THE SEARCH WAS OVER | Slow snow drift, low contrast | Radio static, brief |
| 0:15 | ONE THING STILL WORKED | Sharp cut — tighter, darker | Static resolving to voice |
| 0:30 | DAY EIGHT | Hold, minimal motion | Radio click, then silence |
| 0:45 | THIRTY BELOW | Wind-driven snow | Wind rises |
| 1:00 | THEY WALKED | **Sharp cut** — upward motion | Low tone enters |
| 1:15 | WRONG DIRECTION | Steep ascent, punishing | Wind peaks |
| 1:30 | DAY TEN | **Sharp cut** — green, warm | Wind drops out abruptly |
| 1:45 | SEVENTY TWO DAYS | Slow pull back | Single sustained note |

**Background footage direction:** slow, ambient, low-contrast winter
footage. Nothing with cuts of its own, no fast motion, no text baked in. The
visual's job is to occupy the eye while the ear does the work.

**Avoid:** gameplay footage with UI elements, anything with its own
narrative, anything brightly coloured before the 1:30 turn. The colour shift
at 1:30 is doing real work — it lands harder if everything before it is
cold.

**Three sharp cuts:** 0:15, 1:00, 1:30. Each resets attention at a
structural turn, not at random.

**Overlay check:** no overlay repeats what's being said at that moment. "DAY
EIGHT" appears while the narration describes the broadcast, not while it says
"eighth day."

**Editor settings:**
- Voice: a measured, lower-register narration voice — not an energetic one
- TTS speed: 1.0 (do not speed up; the pauses are load-bearing)
- Export: 1.0
- Background: ambient winter, muted

## Module 6 — Multi-Part Assessment

**Verdict: do not split.**

The natural break is at 1:00, where two survivors decide to walk out.
Splitting there would work structurally — it's a genuine cliffhanger.

But the story's power comes from compression. The whole point is that
seventy two days of ordeal fits into under two minutes, and that
juxtaposition is the emotional engine. Splitting it dilutes exactly what
makes it land.

**The structural element that's missing for a split:** a second independent
mystery. Splittable stories need something unresolved in part one that isn't
just "what happens next" — a question the viewer actively wants answered
rather than an event they want to see. This story has escalation but only one
question, and it's answered by the ending.

**Note the module did its job by refusing.** The prompt explicitly says not
to force a split. A system that always recommends its own most profitable
option isn't a system, it's a sales pitch.

---

## What this run demonstrates

The output is a script you could produce today: 268 words, a beat map with
no gap over 15 seconds, eight overlay cues that don't duplicate narration,
three deliberate visual cuts, and a reasoned decision *not* to split.

Note what the process rejected along the way: three of eight hooks
auto-cut by the rule list, a stronger tier-1 hook passed over for a tier-3
that opened a loop instead of closing one, and a viable split declined
because compression was doing more work than continuation would.

That's the difference between a system and a template. It tells you what
not to do.
