# Escalation Techniques

Purpose: Concrete mechanics for making each beat of a horror story worse than the last, extending [[Pacing]] and [[Suspense_And_Curiosity]] with specific, repeatable techniques.

## Core Principles
- Establish a pattern, then break it. A repeated, ordinary action that goes slightly wrong on a later repetition is more unsettling than an isolated strange event, because the audience has already built an expectation to violate.
- Reveal in ascending sensory proximity: a sound first, then a glimpse, then a partial view, only then a full reveal. Skipping straight to the full reveal spends the scariest resource — not-knowing — too early.
- Alternate false security with escalation. A beat that suggests the danger has passed, followed by a worse beat than before, works harder than continuous escalation with no relief, because the relief makes the next threat land as a violation of an expectation the audience just formed.
- Keep possible outcomes few and clearly bad. Suspense weakens as the number of plausible outcomes grows — narrow the story's implied possibilities rather than leaving them open-ended, and keep them all negative.
- End internal segments on a version of a cliffhanger even when the whole story hasn't reached a part-break — an unresolved sentence, a decision made but not yet explained — sustains tension between the bigger structural beats.

## The Dread Curve
Five phases within a build toward a scare: establish rhythm → disrupt it → sustain the discomfort longer than feels comfortable → release (the scare or reveal) → an aftershock that either closes the moment or re-opens it. Spending the majority of a scene's time in the build, and comparatively little on the release itself, is what makes the release land — a scare with no build reads as random; a build with no release reads as unfinished.

## Key Concepts
- Pattern-then-fracture
- Progressive sensory revelation (sound → glimpse → partial view → full reveal)
- False-security-to-escalation alternation
- Outcome scarcity (few, negative, implied)
- The Dread Curve's five phases
