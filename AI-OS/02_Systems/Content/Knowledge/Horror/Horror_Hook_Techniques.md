## Core Principles
- Concreteness beats vagueness, up to a point. A hook built entirely on mystery ("something strange happened") underperforms one built on a concrete, specific detail with exactly one thing withheld ("I found the third camera in my apartment yesterday. I only own two."). Pure vagueness reads as generic; the strongest hooks are precise about everything except the one thing that matters.
- The threat should be evidenced, not explained. A hook that fully describes the danger resolves the open loop before it can do its job — show the aftermath, the impossible object, the wrong detail; withhold the cause.
- The first line should never be a greeting, a channel intro, or a slow establishing shot. Attention is decided in the first few seconds; anything that isn't the disturbance itself is wasted runway.
- A sudden, unexpected sound or line of on-screen text does real cognitive work at the very start — it interrupts whatever the viewer was doing before they've decided whether to stay. A slow build at the very start gives that decision away for free.

## Four Opening Structures
1. **Concrete-Mundane-Withheld-Element** — ordinary, specific detail (a date, an age, a place) paired with one deliberately missing piece.
2. **Named Specificity** — a specific incident, place, or figure named immediately, rather than a generic setup.
3. **Write Backwards** — decide the ending's reveal first, then construct the opening as the first domino in a chain that leads there, never tipping along the way that the ending is already decided.
4. **Cold Open on a Disturbance** — open on the interruption itself (a sound, a wrong detail, a physical sensation), not on setup leading toward it.

## Key Concepts
- Concreteness-vagueness curve (too vague underperforms; so does over-explaining)
- Evidence of the threat vs. explanation of the threat
- The interrupt as the actual opening move, not a lead-in to one
