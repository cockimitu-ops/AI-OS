## Core Principles
- Curiosity is driven by a perceived gap between what's known and what's wanted — and that gap has to be perceived to work at all. Complete ignorance produces little curiosity, because there's nothing yet to recognize as missing; a small amount of information is what creates the itch, not the absence of information.
- Gap size follows an inverted curve, not a straight line: too small a gap (the answer is obvious) produces boredom; too large a gap (nothing makes sense yet) produces confusion or disengagement rather than curiosity. The strongest position is enough concrete detail to form a real question, with exactly one piece missing.
- Perceived personal relevance overrides the curve. When a gap feels personally important — "this could happen to me" — curiosity stays high even when very little information has been given. This is horror's natural advantage: dread about oneself resists the usual too-large-a-gap penalty other genres face.
- Anticipation of an answer is itself part of what's being chased, not only the answer. This is why a gap that's never going to close, or closes too predictably, loses its pull over time.
- The strongest reveals resolve more than one open question at once. A single piece of information that reframes several earlier details simultaneously produces a stronger response than the same information delivered as isolated answers.

## Key Concepts
- Information gap as the driver of curiosity, not mystery for its own sake
- The inverted-curve calibration (too little / too much information both fail)
- Personal relevance as an override
- Anticipation itself as part of the reward, not just the resolution
- Multi-gap epiphanies (one answer closing several questions at once)
