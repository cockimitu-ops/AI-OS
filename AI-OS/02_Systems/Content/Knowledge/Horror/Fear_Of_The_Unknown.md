# Fear of the Unknown

Purpose: Why unresolved, unexplained threats are more frightening than explained ones, and what that means for how much a horror story should reveal.
Last Updated: 2026-08-03
Status: Active

## Core Principles
- Fear of not knowing appears to be a more fundamental driver of anxiety than fear of specific bad outcomes like pain or death. Once a threat becomes fully understood, it becomes measurably less frightening — the not-knowing is doing real work, not just delaying information.
- A threat that seems to be approaching or gaining specificity produces more persistent unease than a static, already-fully-present one. Movement toward the audience, literal or implied, keeps the threat active rather than letting it settle into something the mind can file away.
- Implication outperforms explanation as a default. A door that's slowly opening is doing more than showing what's behind it would.
- Making the familiar subtly wrong is a distinct, effective technique from introducing something overtly alien — a detail that's almost right produces its own specific unease that an obviously strange thing doesn't.

## Key Concepts
- Understanding a threat reduces its fear value
- Looming/approaching threats sustain unease better than static ones
- Implication over explanation
- Wrongness in the familiar as its own category, distinct from the overtly strange
