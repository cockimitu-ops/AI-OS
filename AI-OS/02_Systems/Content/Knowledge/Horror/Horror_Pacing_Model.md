# Horror Pacing Model

Purpose: Phase-by-phase timing for short-form horror, and the audience-loss curve that makes early pacing decisions disproportionately important.

## Core Principles
- Audience loss is front-loaded, not evenly spread. The large majority of viewers who leave a short-form video do so in its first quarter; losses shrink at each subsequent quartile, then spike again at the very end. A payoff placed only at the true end is seen by a small fraction of the people who started watching — everything earlier has to defend its own quartile, not just set up the finale.
- A single-episode structure has five phases, each with a distinct job: Hook (open the gap) → Setup (establish who and what's at stake) → Escalation (raise the threat, beat by beat) → Climax/Reveal (fill the gap) → Final Sting (reopen a smaller one). The reveal lands most effectively placed before the final stretch, not at the literal end — suspense peaks just before certainty, not at certainty itself.
- Two different rhythms operate simultaneously and shouldn't be confused: a sensory re-engagement cue (a visual change, a sound shift) roughly every few seconds prevents the flat, static feel that causes drop-off; a narrative escalation beat — new information, a shift in stakes — moves on a slower cadence and actually advances the story. The first keeps attention; the second earns it.
- The most common pacing failure isn't too slow or too fast — it's flat. A beat that doesn't escalate or resolve, even if individually well-written, reads as a repeat of the previous beat rather than progress.
- Spoken pace matters independently of visual pacing: a brisker-than-conversational narration speed correlates with stronger early retention, but pushing a TTS voice past what it can render clearly loses more than it gains. The target is brisk and still comprehensible, not maximally fast.

## Applying This to a Multi-Part Structure
This model describes a single, complete arc. A multi-part story (see [[Horror_Story_System]]) needs both layers at once: the overall arc across all parts should still follow Hook→Setup→Escalation→Climax→Sting, while *each individual part* needs its own local hook and its own escalation, since a viewer can land on part 2 without having watched part 1. Reconciling exact per-part timing against this model is not yet done — see `Suggestions.md`.

## Key Concepts
- Front-loaded audience loss (the "quartile churn" problem)
- Reveal placement before, not at, the very end
- Two distinct pacing rhythms: sensory cueing vs. narrative escalation
- Flat beats as the primary pacing failure mode
