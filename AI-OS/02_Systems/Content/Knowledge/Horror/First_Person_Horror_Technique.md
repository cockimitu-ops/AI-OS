## Core Principles
- First-person narration is processed as a placeholder for the audience's own experience, not as someone else's story being reported. This means the narrator's perception, not exposition, is the actual content — a sensory detail ("I heard a scrape on the ceiling") outperforms a stated fact ("there was a noise") because it's the narrator's body doing the noticing, not a narrator describing an event from outside it.
- The narrator's vulnerability is felt as the audience's own, without needing to build sympathy first — there is no distance to close, since there's no separate character to introduce. This is a structural advantage over third-person horror, not just a stylistic choice.
- Whether the narrator's own perception can be trusted is itself a source of tension. Doubt about what's real for the narrator plants doubt for the audience simultaneously.
- Nothing in the story should signal, even briefly, that the narrator doubts their own account before the story is ready to reveal that — a single line that tips the audience early undermines everything built on the narrator's credibility up to that point.
- Physical, checkable evidence — a photo, a recording, an object — gives an information gap something concrete to anchor to, rather than leaving it purely verbal.
- Sentence rhythm should track the narrator's physical state: short and clipped during a scare, longer and sensory-rich when things are calm, so a shift in rhythm alone signals a shift in threat level before the content does.

## Key Concepts
- Vicarious simulation (the narrator's "I" as audience placeholder)
- Sensory perception over exposition
- Unreliable narration as a trust-based tension device
- Never signaling doubt before the story is ready to
- Proof objects as concrete anchors
- Rhythm-as-signal (sentence length tracking tension)
